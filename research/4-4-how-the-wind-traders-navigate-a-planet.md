The physical interplay between elevated surface density ($\rho_0 \approx 1.47\ \text{kg/m}^3$) and lower surface gravity ($g = 7.85\ \text{m/s}^2$) alters the boundary conditions for both aerostatic displacement and aerodynamic flight [16]. The net buoyant lift generated by an aerostat displacing an atmospheric volume $V$ with an internal lifting gas of density $\rho_{gas}$ is defined by Archimedes' principle:

$$F_{buoyant} = \left(\rho_{atm}(z) - \rho_{gas}(z)\right) g V$$

Because Pandoran surface air density is 20% greater than that of Earth, each cubic meter of displaced atmosphere provides a correspondingly greater mass-lifting capacity [16]. Concurrently, the 20% reduction in gravitational acceleration decreases the downward gravitational force acting on any given payload mass $m$:

$$F_g = m g$$

This dual scaling substantially lowers the required ratio of gasbag volume to structural payload mass [16].

However, fluid resistance operating on any airborne body in relative motion scales directly with ambient atmospheric density and the square of relative velocity:

$$F_{drag} = \frac{1}{2} C_D \rho_{atm}(z) A v_{rel}^2$$

where $C_D$ represents the drag coefficient and $A$ denotes the cross-sectional reference area. The denser atmospheric medium penalizes attempts to achieve high airspeed via mechanical or biological thrust [16]. Consequently, long-distance aerial transit on Pandora naturally favors low-airspeed advective routing—navigating through the selective occupation of favorable horizontal wind streams—over brute-force thrust propulsion [1, 4].

## Part B: Planetary Atmospheric Circulation, Jet Dynamics, and Optimal Routing

### Planetary Circulation Engines: Geostrophy, Cell Mechanics, and Synchronous Regimes

Planetary atmospheric circulation is fundamentally driven by meridional imbalances in net radiative flux. Low latitudes absorb an excess of stellar radiation relative to outgoing longwave radiation, whereas polar latitudes experience a net deficit. This latitudinal energy gradient drives a global thermodynamic heat engine transporting internal energy poleward.

The kinematic organization of this flow is governed by planetary rotation through the Coriolis parameter:

$$f = 2\Omega\sin\phi$$

where $\Omega = \frac{2\pi}{\tau_{rot}}$ represents the planetary angular rotation rate and $\phi$ is latitude. The relative importance of rotational forces versus advective inertial accelerations is defined by the non-dimensional Rossby number:

$$Ro = \frac{U}{f L}$$

where $U$ is the characteristic flow velocity and $L$ is the characteristic horizontal length scale.

When $Ro \ll 1$, the Coriolis acceleration dominates over non-linear advection and local accelerations. Under these conditions, the horizontal Navier-Stokes momentum equation simplifies to Geostrophic Balance:

$$f (\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{u}_g) = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla_h p$$

In scalar Cartesian components:

$$u_g = -\frac{1}{\rho f}\frac{\partial p}{\partial y}, \quad v_g = \frac{1}{\rho f}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}$$

Geostrophic balance reveals a counter-intuitive principle of planetary fluid dynamics: large-scale winds do not flow down the pressure gradient from high to low pressure; rather, they blow *parallel* to isobars, oriented such that low pressure is situated to the left of the velocity vector in the Northern Hemisphere and to the right in the Southern Hemisphere (Buys Ballot's Law).

When isobars exhibit substantial curvature, the centrifugal force becomes significant, modifying the flow into Gradient Wind Balance:

$$\frac{V^2}{R} + fV = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial p}{\partial R}$$

where $R$ is the local trajectory radius of curvature ($R > 0$ for cyclonic flow, $R < 0$ for anticyclonic flow).

On a moderately rotating planet with axisymmetric thermal forcing, the latitudinal overturning circulation is structured into three distinct meridional cells:

1. **The Hadley Cell:** A thermally direct circulation where warm equatorial air ascends at the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), flows poleward in the upper troposphere while conserving absolute angular momentum, and descends in the subtropics ($\approx 30^\circ\ \text{latitude}$), driving the surface trade winds via westward Coriolis deflection.
2. **The Ferrel Cell:** A thermally indirect mid-latitude circulation ($30^\circ\text{--}60^\circ$) driven not by mean thermal convection, but as a statistical residual of baroclinic synoptic-scale eddies (weather systems). The surface flow constitutes the prevailing mid-latitude westerlies.
3. **The Polar Cell:** A weak, thermally direct polar cell where cold, dense air sinks at the poles and spreads equatorward, deflected into polar easterlies.

The latitudinal scale and number of zonal jets are governed by the Rhines scale ($L_\beta$) and the Rossby deformation radius ($L_D$):

$$L_\beta = \pi \sqrt{\frac{U}{\beta}}, \quad \beta = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} = \frac{2\Omega\cos\phi}{a}$$

$$L_D = \frac{N H}{f}$$

where $N = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\theta}\frac{\partial \theta}{\partial z}}$ is the Brunt-Väisälä buoyancy frequency, and $H$ is atmospheric scale height. On fast-rotating planets (e.g., Jupiter, Saturn), $L_\beta \ll a$, producing multiple alternating zonal jets. On slowly rotating bodies (e.g., Venus, Titan), $Ro \gg 1$, causing the Hadley cell to extend from equator to pole and generating global atmospheric superrotation [13, 15].

On a tidally locked or synchronously orbiting moon, the non-axisymmetric day-to-night thermal forcing triggers a Matsuno-Gill circulation pattern [13, 14, 15]. Planetary Kelvin waves and off-equatorial Rossby waves create a tilted, chevron-shaped wave structure that channels eddy momentum equatorward, driving a powerful eastward superrotating equatorial jet stream [13, 14, 15].

### Jet Streams, Thermal Wind Dynamics, and Rossby Wave Mechanics

Jet streams are high-velocity wind channels concentrated near the tropopause. Their formation is dictated by the Thermal Wind Balance, derived by combining geostrophic balance with hydrostatic equilibrium ($\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\rho g$) and the ideal gas law ($p = \rho R_d T$):

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}_g}{\partial \ln p} = \frac{R_d}{f}\left(\mathbf{k} \times \nabla_p T\right)$$

In height coordinates:

$$\frac{\partial u_g}{\partial z} \approx -\frac{g}{f \bar{T}}\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}$$

Because the troposphere exhibits a negative meridional temperature gradient ($\frac{\partial T}{\partial y} < 0$, warmer at the equator, colder toward the pole), the eastward geostrophic wind *must* increase with altitude. This shear accumulates throughout the tropospheric depth, reaching its velocity maximum at the tropopause where the meridional temperature gradient weakens and reverses in the stratosphere.

| Core Pressure Altitude | $200\ \text{hPa}$ ($\approx 12\ \text{km}$) | $300\text{--}250\ \text{hPa}$ ($\approx 9\text{--}10.5\ \text{km}$) |
| Typical Core Velocity (Winter / Summer) | $40\text{--}70\ \text{m/s}$ / $25\text{--}40\ \text{m/s}$ | $30\text{--}60\ \text{m/s}$ / $15\text{--}30\ \text{m/s}$ |
| Peak Velocity in Winter Core | $\approx 85\text{--}100\ \text{m/s}$ | $> 110\text{--}130\ \text{m/s}$ |

| Seasonal Latitudinal Migration | Shifts poleward $\sim 5^\circ\text{--}10^\circ$ in summer | Highly dynamic; wanders across $\sim 20^\circ$ latitude |

The polar jet stream meanders continuously through planetary-scale Rossby waves. Rossby waves originate from the conservation of absolute potential vorticity ($PV$):

$$PV = \frac{\zeta + f}{h} = \text{constant}$$

where $\zeta = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}$ is relative vorticity, $f$ is planetary vorticity, and $h$ is fluid layer thickness. Equatorward displacement of an air parcel decreases $f$, forcing an increase in relative vorticity ($\Delta \zeta > 0$, cyclonic curvature) that turns the parcel back poleward. Conversely, poleward displacement increases $f$, forcing anticyclonic turning ($\Delta \zeta < 0$).

For barotropic, non-divergent planetary flow, the linear Rossby wave dispersion relation is:

$$\omega = U k - \frac{\beta k}{k^2 + l^2}$$

which yields the zonal phase velocity:

$$c_x = \frac{\omega}{k} = U - \frac{\beta}{K^2}$$

where $K = \sqrt{k^2 + l^2}$ is the total horizontal wavenumber and $U$ is the mean background zonal flow.

Because $\frac{\beta}{K^2} > 0$, Rossby waves propagate *westward relative to the mean flow*. When the horizontal wavenumber matches the stationary resonant condition:

$$K_s = \sqrt{\frac{\beta}{U}}$$

the wave's phase speed becomes zero ($c_x = 0$), causing the wave pattern to lock into a fixed geographical position.

Large-amplitude stationary waves frequently undergo non-linear wave breaking, forming **blocking anticyclones** (e.g., Omega blocks or Rex blocks). A blocking high creates a persistent, multi-week stagnation of local winds, halting downstream advection and splitting the jet stream into divergent branches that redirect or trap traveling aerostats.

For aerial transit, jet streams present critical operational trade-offs:

* **Advective Acceleration:** An aerostat aligned with the jet axis gains substantial groundspeed, enabling rapid trans-planetary crossings without fuel expenditure.
* **Clear-Air Turbulence (CAT):** The severe horizontal and vertical shear at the margins of the jet core frequently triggers dynamic Kelvin-Helmholtz instability. Instability occurs when the gradient Richardson number drops below its critical threshold:

$$Ri = \frac{N^2}{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}\right)^2} < 0.25$$

This process generates severe, cloudless turbulent breakdown that can structurally compromise large aerostats.

### Vertical Shear, Boundary Layer Turning, and Tactical Orographic Lift

The vertical column of a planetary atmosphere is structured into two hydrodynamic regimes: the Planetary Boundary Layer (PBL, the lowest $1\text{--}2\ \text{km}$), where surface drag and turbulent mixing dominate, and the Free Atmosphere above it.

Within the lowest surface layer ($\approx 100\ \text{m}$), mechanical friction establishes the logarithmic wind profile:

$$u(z) = \frac{u_*}{\kappa}\ln\left(\frac{z}{z_0}\right)$$

where $u_* = \sqrt{\tau_0 / \rho}$ is friction velocity, $\kappa \approx 0.40$ is the von Kármán constant, and $z_0$ is the hydrodynamic roughness length.

Above this layer, the balance between the horizontal Pressure Gradient Force, the Coriolis Force, and vertical turbulent shear stress ($\tau = \rho K_m \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial z}$) creates the **Ekman Spiral**:

$$u(z) = u_g \left(1 - e^{-\gamma z}\cos(\gamma z)\right) - v_g e^{-\gamma z}\sin(\gamma z)$$

$$v(z) = v_g \left(1 - e^{-\gamma z}\cos(\gamma z)\right) + u_g e^{-\gamma z}\sin(\gamma z)$$

where $\gamma = \sqrt{\frac{f}{2 K_m}}$ is the inverse boundary layer depth scale ($h_{Ekman} = \pi/\gamma$).

As an aerostat ascends from the surface through the Ekman layer, the wind vector accelerates and rotates (veering clockwise with height in the Northern Hemisphere, backing counter-clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere), turning through $15^\circ\text{--}45^\circ$ before aligning with free geostrophic flow.

Above the boundary layer, baroclinic thermal advection creates strong directional wind shear across the free troposphere:

* **Veering with Height ($\frac{\partial \theta_{wind}}{\partial z} > 0$):** Indicates warm thermal advection ($\mathbf{u}_g \cdot \nabla T < 0$).
* **Backing with Height ($\frac{\partial \theta_{wind}}{\partial z} < 0$):** Indicates cold thermal advection ($\mathbf{u}_g \cdot \nabla T > 0$).

Furthermore, nocturnal boundary-layer decoupling often triggers inertial oscillations that produce **Low-Level Jets (LLJs)**: wind maxima occurring at altitudes of only $300\text{--}800\ \text{m}$ with speeds exceeding $20\text{--}30\ \text{m/s}$ (e.g., the Great Plains LLJ via the Blackadar mechanism). Over tropical latitudes, equatorial stratospheric winds exhibit the **Quasi-Biennial Oscillation (QBO)**, where downward-propagating zonal wind regimes completely reverse between eastward and westward flow every $\approx 28\ \text{months}$.

At the meso-scale, airflow encountering topography generates **Orographic Mountain Waves**. The vertical propagation of gravity waves is governed by the Scorer Parameter ($l^2$):

$$l^2(z) = \frac{N^2(z)}{U^2(z)} - \frac{1}{U(z)}\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial z^2}$$

When $l^2(z)$ decreases rapidly with height ($\frac{\partial l^2}{\partial z} < 0$, typically due to wind speed increasing with altitude), gravity wave energy is trapped within the lower troposphere, forming stationary **trapped lee waves** and dangerous **rotors**—closed turbulent vortices that form beneath wave crests.

Conversely, when $l^2(z)$ remains positive and smooth throughout the atmosphere, gravity waves propagate vertically into the upper stratosphere, growing in amplitude as density decays ($\rho(z) = \rho_0 e^{-z/H}$).

These stratospheric mountain waves were leveraged by the **Airbus Perlan Mission II** sailplane, which soared engineless to a record pressure altitude of $76,124\ \text{ft}$ ($23,203\ \text{m}$) over the Patagonian Andes on September 2, 2018, by riding mountain wave lift coupled directly to the polar night jet [17, 18, 19, 20].

### Non-Instrumental Navigation and Ethnographic Precedents

Planetary-scale navigation without digital or mechanical instrumentation is an established historical reality. Traditional wayfinding frameworks integrate astronomical reference frames, swell diffraction patterns, and traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) into precise, error-correcting mental models.

In the Polynesian and Micronesian traditions documented by David Lewis (*We, the Navigators*), Thomas Gladwin (*East is a Big Bird*), and Ben Finney, wayfinding is formulated as a continuous vector integration problem:

* **The Sidereal Star Compass:** Navigators utilize the invariant rising and setting azimuths of stars to establish an absolute heading reference independent of local drift.
* **The *Etak* System:** Navigators conceptualize the vessel as stationary at the center of the horizon, while an out-of-sight reference island (*etak*) moves backward beneath fixed celestial reference stars as the ocean moves past.
* **Biological Homeward Indicators:** Pelagic bird flights (such as the fairy tern *Gygis alba* and boobies *Sula*, which fly seaward at dawn and return to roost at dusk within a strict $30\text{--}50\ \text{km}$ coastal radius) provide reliable terminal homing vectors.

In the Indian Ocean, mariners organized international trade around the monsoon wind cycle. Summer heating over the Asian landmass generates a continental low-pressure system, drawing the moist Southwest Monsoon across the ocean from June to September. In winter, continental cooling establishes a high-pressure system, reversing the flow into the dry Northeast Monsoon from November to March.

Similarly, Portuguese mariners developed the *Volta do mar* ("turn of the sea"): discovering that sailing directly north against the Northeast Trade winds was impossible, they sailed northwest into the open Atlantic until intercepting the mid-latitude westerlies, which carried them eastward back to Europe.

On a tidally locked or synchronously orbiting moon, celestial navigation is uniquely adapted:

* **The Primary Gas Giant as an Invariant Anchor:** The primary planet (Polyphemus) remains fixed at a constant zenith angle and azimuth for any given geographic location on the moon's near-side, serving as an immediate reference for both latitude and longitude [16].
* **Sibling Moons as an Ephemeris Clock:** The orbital transits, occultations, and phase cycles of sibling moons (Cassandra, Hades, Dante) function as a celestial ephemeris clock, allowing navigators to compute precise local time and seasonal phase [16].

### Weather Routing as an Optimal Control Problem

The mathematical theory of navigating through moving fluid fields was established by Ernst Zermelo (1931). **Zermelo's Navigation Problem** seeks the time-optimal trajectory $\mathbf{x}(t) = (x(t), y(t))$ for a vehicle operating in a time-varying drift field $\mathbf{W}(x, y, t) = (W_x, W_y)$ with constant own-airspeed $V$ [23, 24, 25, 27]:

$$\dot{x} = V \cos\theta(t) + W_x(x, y, t)$$

$$\dot{y} = V \sin\theta(t) + W_y(x, y, t)$$

Applying Pontryagin's Maximum Principle (PMP) to minimize total transit time $T = \int_0^T dt$ yields Zermelo's Steering Control Law [24, 26, 27]:

$$\dot{\theta} = -\frac{\partial W_x}{\partial y}\sin^2\theta + \left(\frac{\partial W_x}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial W_y}{\partial y}\right)\sin\theta\cos\theta + \frac{\partial W_y}{\partial x}\cos^2\theta$$

In the degenerate regime where vehicle own-airspeed approaches zero ($V \to 0$), the vehicle lacks horizontal thrust authority relative to the surrounding air [24]. Instead, control authority is transferred entirely to the vertical dimension:

$$\dot{\mathbf{x}}_h(t) = \mathbf{W}_h(x, y, z(t), t)$$

$$\text{Subject to Control: } u(t) = \dot{z}(t) \in [-w_{max}, w_{max}]$$

Because atmospheric wind vectors shear and rotate with height ($\frac{\partial \mathbf{W}_h}{\partial z} \neq 0$), two-dimensional horizontal navigation is achieved solely by selecting vertical flight levels [21, 22].

In Google's Project Loon, long-duration stratospheric station-keeping and trans-oceanic navigation were executed entirely without horizontal propulsion [21, 22].

Using deep reinforcement learning (RL) formulated over high-resolution numerical weather models, the autonomous flight computer continuously selected vertical flight levels to intercept sheared winds [21, 22]. The RL policy discovered sophisticated tactical maneuvers—including circular holding patterns, figure-eight loops, and alternating vertical drift tacking—outperforming classical flight-control algorithms [21, 22].

In practical buoyancy systems, vertical maneuvering is constrained by an **irreversible resource budget**:

* **Ascent Cost:** Requires the shedding of expendable mass (water ballast release) or thermal energy expenditure [4].
* **Descent Cost:** Requires the irrecoverable venting of lifting gas ($H_2$ or $He$) [5, 18].

Every vertical excursion permanently consumes ballast or lifting volume, converting weather routing into a constrained optimal control problem:

$$\min_{z(t)} \int_0^T dt \quad \text{subject to} \quad \int_0^T \left[ c_b \max(0, \dot{z}) + c_v \max(0, -\dot{z}) \right] dt \le \mathcal{B}_{total}$$

where $c_b$ and $c_v$ are the resource cost coefficients for ballast dumping and gas venting, and $\mathcal{B}_{total}$ is the finite initial resource capacity.

## Conclusions

The semi-annual circumnavigation of Pandora by the Tlalim Clan illustrates that aerial wayfinding without digital sensors or mechanical propulsion is an empirical, quantitative science [4].

Operating within an atmosphere characterized by 20% higher density and 20% lower surface gravity than Earth, the Tlalim combine biological lifting bodies (Medusoids) with neural-bonded draft organisms (Windrays) to achieve planetary transit [1, 4, 10, 11, 16, 18].

Because horizontal thrust cannot overcome planetary-scale wind fields, navigation reduces to a three-dimensional optimization problem: selecting vertical levels within a sheared, baroclinic atmosphere where wind velocity and direction vary systematically with altitude [10, 21, 22].

Through generational, orally transmitted cognitive maps—analogous to Polynesian sidereal star compasses, the historical exploitation of monsoon reversals, and the Atlantic *Volta do mar*—the Tlalim decode planetary waves, boundary-layer Ekman turning, and orographic mountain waves [4, 9].

Their migration patterns represent a cultural solution to Zermelo's navigation problem, validating the principle that an engineless craft can achieve global trajectories by using altitude as a directional steering mechanism [10, 21, 22, 24].
