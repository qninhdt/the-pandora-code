# Deep Research - Pandora's smallest things (pandoras-smallest-things)

## Goal

This chapter teaches **two payloads at once**:

- **Pandora payload:** the Avatar films make Pandora look alive at the scale of
  eyes, wings, roots, and glowing leaves, but every one of those visible systems
  depends on an almost entirely unseen world. The chapter asks what official canon
  actually establishes about **Pandora's microbes, fungi-like decomposers, soil,
  parasites, pathogens, disease, decay, and host-associated microbiomes**—and, just
  as importantly, where canon says nothing. It should treat the forest floor as a
  living reactor rather than inert scenery, and ask what must happen to fallen
  leaves, dung, carcasses, and dead giants if Pandora's extraordinary productivity
  is to continue. The arrival of Earth organisms creates a second question: could
  organisms from two independently evolved biospheres infect one another, exchange
  microbes, or merely poison one another by accident? Research must distinguish
  what the films or official companion sources show from what biology lets us infer.
- **STEM payload:** real **soil ecology and host–pathogen co-evolution**. Soil is an
  organised, spatially patchy ecosystem where bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists,
  viruses, microarthropods, roots, water films, minerals, and dead organic matter
  continuously rebuild fertility. Hosts are ecosystems too, carrying microbiomes
  that digest, defend, signal, and sometimes turn pathogenic. Across generations,
  hosts and pathogens change one another's selective landscape through
  **negative frequency-dependent selection**, gene-for-gene or matching-alleles
  interactions, immune escape, resistance costs, and fluctuating selection. The
  chapter's central evolutionary lens is the **Red Queen hypothesis**: populations
  may have to keep evolving simply to maintain their relative fitness against
  enemies that evolve back. The chapter must explain what the hypothesis does and
  does not claim, why it became one major explanation for the maintenance of sex,
  and where evidence remains mixed.

Research must cover BOTH sides thoroughly and keep them separable. Breadth is
welcome—the author will filter aggressively.

**Scope guard (avoid overlap with neighbouring chapters):** This chapter is about
**the invisible ecology that recycles a forest and the evolutionary chase between
hosts and their biological enemies**. Do **NOT**:

- re-run **mycorrhizal networks, carbon transfer between trees, common mycorrhizal
  networks, or the “wood-wide web” debate**—that was “The wood wide web.” Fungi and
  root-associated microbes belong here as members of a rhizosphere and decomposer
  community, but no network-signalling primer and no claims that forests behave as
  altruistic superorganisms.
- re-run **alternative biochemistries, chirality, non-carbon life, genetic-code
  alternatives, or astrobiology's definition of life**—that was “What is Pandoran
  life made of.” Take biochemical uncertainty as a given. Revisit it only for one
  sharply bounded question: what molecular compatibility would cross-world infection
  require?
- re-run **phylogenetics, cladograms, parsimony, or reconstruction of Pandora's tree
  of life**—that was “Pandoran tree of life.” Co-evolution here means reciprocal
  selection through time, not drawing the planet's phylogeny.
- re-run **atmospheric chemistry, oxygen/methane disequilibrium, biosignatures, or
  why humans need exo-packs**—that was “What's in the air.” A mask is not evidence
  of microbial danger unless an official source explicitly says so.
- re-run **the planetary Eywa network, distributed cognition, information theory,
  percolation, or Gaia**—Part III owns those subjects. A microbial community may be
  called a network only in the ordinary ecological sense; do not turn it into a
  miniature Eywa.
- re-run **forest stratification, tree-height physics, or Hometree as a foundation
  species / structural habitat**—the previous chapters own those concepts. Fallen
  wood and leaf litter appear here only as substrates that decomposers transform.
- turn the chapter into a generic **human pandemic history** or a catalogue of
  famous diseases. Use a few discriminating cases to reveal evolutionary mechanisms.
- moralise first-contact, colonialism, or indigenous research ethics—Part VIII owns
  that discussion. Planetary protection belongs here only as the technical problem
  of preventing forward and backward biological contamination.

## Part A - Pandora canon

Research what official Avatar material establishes about **Pandora below the scale
of the familiar animals and plants: its soils, decomposers, microbes, parasites,
diseases, and biological contact with Earth**. Be specific, cite sources, and
distinguish official canon (the films *Avatar* (2009), *Avatar: The Way of Water*
(2022), published screenplays/scripts, *James Cameron's Avatar: An Activist Survival
Guide* by Wilhelm & Mathison, *The World of Avatar: A Visual Exploration*, *The Wild
Life of Pandora* by Dinda, Pandorapedia and other Cameron/Lightstorm-sanctioned
material) from licensed game/comic continuity and from fan-wiki/community claims.
If using *Avatar: Frontiers of Pandora*, comics, or role-playing/game guides, state
their exact official status rather than silently treating every licensed detail as
film canon.

1. **Does canon name or show microorganisms at all?** Find every official reference
   to bacteria, archaea, microscopic algae, plankton, spores, yeasts, molds,
   fungi-like organisms, protists, viruses, bacteriophages, microscopic parasites,
   or other life too small to see. Search both explicit text and production/companion
   descriptions, but separate an organism that merely *looks fungal* from one an
   official source actually classifies as a fungus or microbe. For each candidate,
   report its Na'vi/common/scientific-style name if given, habitat, ecological role,
   and source. If canon has no confirmed bacterium, virus, or microbial taxon, say so
   plainly; absence is a result, not a defect to fill with fan invention.
2. **What lies underfoot?** Gather every canonical description or image-supported
   observation of Pandoran soil and the forest floor: leaf litter, humus-like layers,
   mud, peat, suspended/canopy soil, mineral substrate, root mats, fungal-looking
   growth, rot, and the colour or texture of disturbed ground. Look for scenes after
   rain, beneath Hometree, around the Tree of Souls, in logged/burned terrain, the
   Hallelujah Mountains, coastal forests, reef sediments, and the ash around RDA
   clearances. Distinguish “soil” from raw regolith or mining spoil. Does any source
   describe soil depth, fertility, chemistry, acidity, moisture, or organic content?
   Does canon ever explain how soil remains fertile under such dense, fast-growing
   vegetation?
3. **Where do the dead things go?** What official material says or shows about the
   decomposition of fallen leaves, wood, dung, shed skin, carcasses, and other
   detritus. Identify any named scavengers, detritivores, carrion feeders,
   dung-feeders, wood-borers, saprophytes, or decomposer guilds, including small
   visible animals that may connect the microscopic and macroscopic food webs. Ask
   whether dead organisms visibly decay at Earth-like rates, whether the Na'vi bury
   or expose bodies, and what happens materially after a body is returned to Eywa.
   Keep ceremony separate from biochemical decomposition. If the films jump from
   death to disappearance without showing decay, record that as a high-value gap.
4. **Nutrient cycling and productivity.** Search official sources for claims about
   nitrogen fixation, nutrient uptake, soil renewal, carbon storage, phosphorus,
   decomposition speed, forest productivity, or the ecological consequences of
   Pandora's lower gravity, warm wet climate, and high atmospheric carbon dioxide.
   Do not infer mechanisms merely because the forest is lush. Report which parts are
   explicitly stated and which are visual inference. Check whether the planet's
   electrical/root connectivity is ever said to transport *nutrients*, rather than
   only information or memories; do not collapse those into the same claim.
5. **Host-associated symbionts and microbiomes.** Does canon say that Na'vi,
   avatars, tulkun, other fauna, or plants carry gut, skin, respiratory, reproductive,
   root, or surface symbionts? Are digestion, fermentation, scent, bioluminescence,
   immunity, or development ever attributed to microbial partners? Investigate any
   official claims that Pandoran organisms share cells, neural tissue, DNA, or
   microbes through *tsaheylu*, while treating “data connection” and “microbial
   transfer” as entirely separate unless a source explicitly connects them. Ask
   whether the avatar programme had to engineer a microbiome, whether avatar bodies
   eat and digest native food without difficulty, and whether humans eating Pandoran
   food is shown or discussed.
6. **Disease, infection, parasites, and immunity within Pandora.** Build a sourced
   inventory of any canonically documented illness in Na'vi, tulkun, terrestrial
   fauna, plants, or reef organisms. Look for fever, infected wounds, epidemics,
   wasting, blights, parasites, brood parasitism, blood-feeding, fungal disease,
   viral disease, or unexplained mass mortality. Separate:

   - an **infectious disease** caused by a transmissible organism;
   - a **parasite** that lives on or in a host;
   - **venom, poison, allergen, toxin, or caustic chemistry**;
   - trauma, starvation, radiation, atmospheric exposure, and other non-infectious
     injury;
   - spiritual or metaphorical language that is not a medical diagnosis.

   Gather what official sources say about Na'vi immune biology, healing, wound care,
   medicinal plants, antisepsis, and whether Pandoran animals show scars or recovery
   from infection. If canon depicts wounds healing but never mentions infection,
   mark the silence instead of assuming immunity.
7. **Earth life meeting Pandoran life.** What films and official materials establish
   about quarantine, decontamination, medical screening, vaccination, sterile
   facilities, sample containment, food safety, or planetary-protection protocols at
   Hell's Gate, Bridgehead, aboard the ISVs, and in the avatar laboratories. Did
   humans deliberately or accidentally bring Earth bacteria, fungi, crops, pests,
   livestock, or waste organisms to Pandora? Are human habitats biologically closed,
   or do personnel routinely touch, eat, breathe near, and carry native material?
   Are humans ever infected by a Pandoran organism, and are Na'vi or native organisms
   ever infected by an Earth organism? Verify recurring community claims such as
   “Pandoran pathogens cannot infect humans” or “humans have no immunity” against an
   actual official source. Do not mistake atmospheric toxicity for infection risk.
8. **The special case of avatars and recombinants.** What canon establishes about
   the immune and microbial consequences of bodies combining human and Na'vi genetic
   material: immune rejection, tissue compatibility, gestation, gut colonisation,
   exposure to both biospheres, laboratory sterility, or infection vulnerability.
   Does a remotely operated avatar have its own immune system and resident microbes?
   Does consciousness transfer change anything biological? Keep the focus on host
   ecology and infection; neural-interface mechanics and personal identity belong to
   later chapters.
9. **Disturbance of the invisible ecosystem.** Search for canonical evidence about
   what RDA fire, bulldozing, mining, road-building, explosives, herbicides,
   pollutants, or Bridgehead construction do to soil and small life. Is soil burned,
   compacted, removed, sterilised, contaminated, or replaced? Does reclamation ever
   occur? Are Earth microbes plausible stowaways in wastewater or agriculture
   according to an official source? Keep the emphasis on physical observations and
   stated procedures, not the later ethical verdict.
10. **Na'vi knowledge of the small and unseen.** Does Na'vi language or traditional
    ecological knowledge include named concepts for soil, decay, mold, infection,
    contagion, parasites, fermentation, rot, medicine, or tiny organisms? Do healers
    such as Mo'at diagnose infectious illness or use practices that would alter
    microbial growth? Cite Paul Frommer's official language material or sanctioned
    lexicons where possible and distinguish a lexical item from evidence that Na'vi
    possess a modern germ theory.
11. **Where canon is silent or contradictory—list and rank the gaps.** Likely
    candidates to verify include: no named bacterium or virus; no explicit account of
    decomposition; no soil profile or nutrient budget; no host microbiome; no native
    infectious disease; no infection-control procedure despite extensive biological
    contact; no answer on whether Earth and Pandoran pathogens can cross hosts; no
    explanation of how avatar bodies acquire symbionts; no account of introduced
    Earth microbes; and imagery that resembles fungi despite no official taxonomy.
    Rank gaps by how much they matter to the chapter. Give contradictions and source
    tiers rather than reconciling them by guesswork.

## Part B - Earth-science literature (soil microbiomes, host–pathogen co-evolution, and the Red Queen)

Explain the real science from credible and preferably primary sources at a level a
curious non-specialist can follow. Named experiments, quantitative ranges, and dates
are strongly preferred. Where a popular simplification is disputed, show the dispute.

1. **Soil is a habitat, not a substance.** Define soil as a structured mixture of
   mineral particles, organic matter, water, gases, roots, and organisms arranged in
   aggregates, pores, horizons, and microsites. Explain how pore size, water films,
   oxygen gradients, pH, temperature, mineral surfaces, and root inputs create
   radically different habitats millimetres apart. Give defensible orders of
   magnitude for microbial abundance and diversity in a gram of soil, while
   distinguishing cells, operational taxonomic units/amplicon sequence variants, and
   actual species. Trace famous figures such as “billions of microorganisms” or
   “thousands to millions of species per gram” to their methods and flag overclaims.
   Cover why most environmental microbes were historically called “unculturable,”
   what the **great plate count anomaly** means, and how metagenomics changed the
   picture.
2. **The soil food web.** Map the functional cast without turning it into a taxonomic
   catalogue: bacteria and archaea; saprotrophic and symbiotic fungi; protists that
   graze microbes; nematodes; mites and springtails; earthworms and other ecosystem
   engineers; viruses and phages; plant roots; and larger detritivores. Explain how
   energy and nutrients move through bacterial and fungal channels, the **microbial
   loop**, trophic release of mineral nutrients, and why the smallest organisms
   cannot be understood in isolation from their predators. Include evidence for
   functional redundancy versus specialised, irreplaceable functions.
3. **Decomposition: turning a corpse back into an ecosystem.** Walk through the
   degradation of soluble compounds, cellulose, hemicellulose, chitin, proteins,
   lipids, and lignin; extracellular enzymes; succession among decomposer guilds;
   immobilisation versus mineralisation; and controls imposed by temperature,
   moisture, oxygen, substrate quality, and nutrient ratios. Explain why warm and wet
   does not automatically mean infinitely fast decay, and why waterlogging or anoxia
   can preserve carbon. Cover modern revisions to the old idea that chemically
   “recalcitrant humus” persists by itself: the roles of **microbial necromass**,
   mineral association, aggregate occlusion, and the **MEMS framework** in persistent
   soil organic matter. Provide memorable quantitative examples—turnover times,
   microbial carbon-use efficiency, or how much soil carbon is microbial necromass—
   only where literature supports them.
4. **Nutrient cycles run through microbial gates.** Explain nitrogen fixation,
   ammonification, nitrification, denitrification, anammox, phosphorus solubilisation,
   sulfur transformations, methane production/oxidation, and how microbes make
   nutrients available or return them to the atmosphere. Focus on mechanisms a reader
   can carry away, not a list of chemical equations. Identify the limiting nutrients
   typical of old tropical soils versus young volcanic soils, and explain how a lush
   forest may hold much of its nutrient capital in living biomass rather than in
   deep, rich soil. Include classic ecosystem-scale evidence from litter
   decomposition, fertilisation, and chronosequence studies.
5. **The rhizosphere and the economy around a root.** Define the rhizosphere and
   explain root exudates, mucilage, sloughed cells, microbial recruitment, nutrient
   mobilisation, plant-growth-promoting bacteria, nitrogen-fixing symbioses, and
   disease-suppressive soils. Cover the “rhizosphere effect” quantitatively where
   possible and the idea that plants alter microbial gene expression and community
   composition. Treat the **holobiont** and “second genome” language critically:
   when is the host-plus-microbiome a useful unit, and when does it overstate
   cooperation or stable inheritance? Mention mycorrhizae only as one rhizosphere
   partnership; do not re-open the common-network controversy.
6. **Microbiomes are neither decoration nor universally beneficial.** Explain
   colonisation resistance, digestion, vitamin production, immune education,
   metabolite signalling, and the continuum from mutualist through commensal to
   opportunistic pathogen. Show how context—diet, host genotype, antibiotics,
   inflammation, environment—can flip an interaction. Cover dysbiosis carefully:
   distinguish correlation from cause and avoid presenting a single “healthy
   microbiome” as universal. Ask for strong causal examples from gnotobiotic animals,
   faecal microbiota transplantation for recurrent *C. difficile*, plant synthetic
   communities, or other well-controlled systems.
7. **What makes a pathogen able to infect a host?** At the mechanistic level, cover
   exposure route, attachment/receptor compatibility, entry, access to usable
   nutrients, replication machinery, temperature and chemistry, evasion or
   manipulation of immunity, transmission to the next host, and ecological dose.
   Explain why host range varies from extremely narrow phages to broad generalists,
   and why a microbe can be harmless in one tissue or host yet pathogenic in another.
   Distinguish infection, disease, virulence, pathogenicity, spillover, reservoir,
   vector, and zoonosis. Use a small set of revealing host-jump cases rather than a
   pandemic catalogue.
8. **The evolutionary chase.** Build the conceptual path from natural selection to
   reciprocal co-evolution:

   - host resistance alleles rise because infection removes susceptible hosts;
   - pathogen infectivity or immune-escape alleles rise because resistant hosts are
     now common;
   - resistance and infectivity have costs and trade-offs;
   - rare host genotypes may gain an advantage precisely because pathogens are
     adapted to common ones;
   - spatial mosaics, migration, mutation, recombination, generation-time
     differences, and environmental variation prevent one simple global arms race.

   Compare **arms-race dynamics** (successive directional escalation) with
   **fluctuating selection / negative frequency-dependent selection** (alleles cycle
   because today's winner becomes tomorrow's common target). Explain the
   **gene-for-gene model** from plant pathology, **matching-alleles models**, and the
   geographic mosaic theory of co-evolution. Ask for time-series or experimental
   evolution studies that actually demonstrate reciprocal change rather than merely
   assuming it.
9. **The Red Queen hypothesis—origin, meaning, and competing versions.** Trace the
   name to the Red Queen in Lewis Carroll's *Through the Looking-Glass* and the
   evolutionary metaphor to **Leigh Van Valen's 1973 paper “A New Evolutionary Law.”**
   Distinguish Van Valen's broad macroevolutionary claim about roughly constant
   extinction probability from later **biotic Red Queen** models of antagonistic
   co-evolution and from the specifically **sexual-selection/maintenance-of-sex Red
   Queen hypothesis** associated with W. D. Hamilton, John Jaenike, Graham Bell, and
   others. State the hypothesis precisely: recombination may continually generate
   rare host genotypes that escape currently common parasites. It does **not** mean
   that every organism constantly improves, that all evolution is progress, or that
   every host–pathogen interaction is an endless escalation.
10. **Does the Red Queen explain sex? Evidence and limits.** Cover why sex is costly
    (the twofold cost of males and the breakup of successful gene combinations), how
    parasite-mediated selection could offset that cost, and what predictions follow:
    sex/recombination should be favoured where parasite pressure and genotype-specific
    infection are high; common host clones should be targeted; parasite populations
    should track host genotypes through time. Seek the best evidence and counterevidence:

    - **New Zealand freshwater snails (*Potamopyrgus antipodarum*) and trematodes**—
      Lively and colleagues' spatial/temporal work on sexual versus asexual lineages;
    - **Daphnia–Pasteuria**, flax–flax rust, wild plant–pathogen systems, and
      experimental *Caenorhabditis elegans–Serratia marcescens* co-evolution;
    - microbial/phage experimental evolution where recombination or diversity alters
      resistance;
    - alternative and complementary explanations for sex, including mutation
      clearance/Muller's ratchet, Fisher–Muller effects, variable environments, and
      DNA repair.

    Explain criticisms: publication bias, difficulty distinguishing Red Queen
    predictions from other fluctuating-selection models, systems where parasites
    favour clonality or selfing, and the fact that sex predates many present host–
    pathogen associations. Say where the evidence is strong, suggestive, or weak.
11. **Disease ecology in a community and in soil.** Explain environmental reservoirs,
    sapronotic pathogens, density- versus frequency-dependent transmission,
    superspreading, host competence, vectors, and how community composition alters
    transmission. Treat the **dilution effect** debate honestly: biodiversity can
    reduce risk in some systems but can amplify or leave it unchanged in others;
    mechanism and scale matter. Cover how soil suppressiveness, microbial competition,
    predation, phages, and resource limitation can constrain a pathogen, while
    disturbance, monoculture, warming, or nutrient pulses can release it. Use plant
    disease and wildlife examples especially relevant to a forest ecosystem.
12. **Could an alien microbe infect an Earth organism?** Analyse this as a nested
    compatibility problem, not a yes/no slogan. An independently evolved microbe
    might fail because it cannot recognise receptors, read molecular signals, use the
    host's nutrients, tolerate its temperature/pH, evade immunity, or reproduce with
    unfamiliar biochemistry. Even shared carbon/water/DNA-like chemistry would not
    guarantee host compatibility. Conversely, organisms need not infect to cause
    harm: toxins, allergens, corrosive metabolites, oxygen consumption, biofouling,
    ecological competition, or unchecked growth in a vacant niche could matter.
    Compare this reasoning with host specificity on Earth, cross-kingdom pathogens,
    broad-host-range microbes, invasive species, and naïve-host epidemics. Discuss
    chirality or genetic-code mismatch only as compatibility filters, not as a repeat
    of alternative-biochemistry theory. Flag the lack of empirical alien-life data.
13. **Planetary protection and the contamination experiment.** Summarise forward
    contamination, backward contamination, sterilisation, clean rooms, bioburden,
    restricted Earth return, and COSPAR/NASA planetary-protection categories. Use
    concrete cases—the Viking landers' sterilisation, Mars spacecraft clean-room
    protocols, Apollo quarantine and its limitations, sample-return planning,
    extremophiles surviving clean rooms or simulated space exposure—to show how hard
    “sterile” is. Distinguish protecting scientific measurements, protecting an alien
    ecosystem, and protecting Earth. Ask what a competent astrobiologist would sample
    first on Pandora and what controls would be required to prove an organism is
    native rather than spacecraft contamination.
14. **How we would study Pandora's invisible biosphere.** Explain a rigorous field
    workflow a reader can visualise: replicate soil cores across horizons and
    habitats; sterile blanks and contamination controls; microscopy and cell counts;
    culturing with its known bias; 16S/18S/ITS-like marker surveys only if homologous
    molecules exist; shotgun metagenomics; metatranscriptomics and metaproteomics;
    stable-isotope probing; enzyme assays; metabolomics; viral metagenomics;
    single-cell methods; and manipulative litter/decomposition experiments. Explain
    what each method reveals and what it cannot. For a suspected pathogen, update
    **Koch's postulates** with molecular criteria and ethical/ecological constraints.
    Emphasise that Earth primers, databases, stains, and culture media might all miss
    genuinely alien biology.
15. **Good analogies and testable inferences connecting the science back to
    Pandora.** Ask the researcher to evaluate, not merely repeat, hypotheses such as:

    - a warm, wet, productive Pandoran forest should have extremely rapid nutrient
      turnover, but its soil need not be nutrient-rich because nutrients may be
      captured almost immediately by living biomass;
    - conspicuous glow, giant animals, and neural interfaces tell us almost nothing
      about the organisms that mineralise a fallen leaf; the smallest life could be
      the largest canonical blind spot;
    - if Pandoran hosts reproduce sexually and carry fast-evolving parasites,
      Red-Queen dynamics are plausible, but neither fact has been sufficiently
      established by “the forest looks diverse”;
    - a planet-spanning biological network would not abolish host–pathogen conflict;
      shared connectivity could aid defence, aid transmission, do both, or be
      irrelevant depending on mechanism—canon must not be replaced with metaphor;
    - Earth and Pandora may be too biochemically or ecologically mismatched for true
      infection, while still being dangerously compatible at the level of toxins,
      nutrient theft, surface colonisation, or ecosystem invasion;
    - avatar bodies are the most informative natural experiment because they combine
      material from one lineage with a body expected to live, eat, heal, and make
      contact in the other biosphere—yet canon may never say how their microbial
      community is established;
    - the cleanest way to discover Pandora is not to start with a giant animal but to
      follow one labelled atom of carbon or nitrogen from litter through microbes,
      soil fauna, roots, and new leaf.

    For each, identify what is supported by canon, what is ordinary biological
    inference, what is speculation, and what observation or experiment would falsify
    it.

## Output requirements

- Cite sources inline and provide a usable bibliography with direct links/DOIs where
  possible. Prefer official Avatar material over fan wikis, and primary or high-quality
  review literature over unsourced summaries.
- Keep **Part A (Pandora canon)** and **Part B (Earth science)** clearly separated.
  Keep the ranked canon-gap list from A11 as its own explicit section; the chapter is
  built as much from honest silence as from facts.
- For every Pandora claim, label the source tier: **film/script, official companion,
  licensed continuity, community/fan source, or inference from imagery**. Never let a
  detailed wiki entry become canon by repetition.
- For science, prioritise foundational and current work on soil microbial ecology,
  decomposition, and co-evolution—for example **Torsvik, Gans, Fierer, Bardgett, van
  der Heijden, Philippot, Rillig, Lehmann & Kleber, Cotrufo, Liang, Bever, Foster,
  Koskella, Thompson, Brockhurst, Woolhouse, Lively, Hamilton, and Van Valen**—while
  verifying the exact relevance and claims of each source rather than name-dropping.
- Flag uncertainty and common errors explicitly: treating soil as inert dirt;
  equating abundance reads with active organisms; assuming all microbes can be
  cultured; treating all fungi as one cooperative network; assuming every microbiome
  association is causal or beneficial; using “dysbiosis” without a testable baseline;
  confusing infection with disease or poison; describing any reciprocal selection as
  a Red Queen; presenting arms races and negative frequency-dependent cycles as the
  same dynamic; claiming the Red Queen is the single settled explanation for sex; or
  saying alien infection is either impossible or inevitable.
- Where a number varies with method—cells or taxa per gram of soil, soil-carbon
  residence time, microbial contribution to persistent organic matter, host-range
  breadth, recombination advantage—give the range, units, environment, method, and
  source instead of one universal figure.
- Include concrete, lay-readable anchors where justified: the number of cells in a
  gram of soil; the scale of a water-filled pore; a litterbag experiment; a labelled
  nitrogen atom moving through a food web; a resistant genotype becoming common and
  therefore becoming the pathogen's new target; sexual and clonal snails facing
  trematodes; and the contamination controls around a soil core from another world.
- Respect the scope guard. Mycorrhizal-network claims, alternative-biochemistry
  surveys, phylogenetic reconstruction, atmospheric biosignatures, Eywa's network,
  forest architecture, and first-contact ethics already have—or will have—their own
  chapters. Here they are only boundaries around **soil ecology and the evolutionary
  contest between hosts and pathogens**.
