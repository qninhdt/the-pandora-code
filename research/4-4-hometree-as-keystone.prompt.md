# Deep Research - Hometree as keystone (hometree-as-keystone)

## Goal

This chapter teaches **two payloads at once**:

- **Pandora payload:** **Hometree (*Kelutral*)** is not merely the building the
  Omatikaya live in - it is a **single organism that is an entire habitat**, and its
  felling by the RDA is the whole Avatar saga's cleanest natural experiment in what
  happens when you delete the structure a community is built around. The chapter's
  job is to read the fall of Hometree as an **ecological event**, not only as a human
  atrocity: what exactly was lost when a 300-metre, ~20,000-year-old tree came down,
  which organisms lost their world along with the clan, what could regrow and on what
  timescale, and why the surrounding forest is not simply "one tree short." The
  chapter must also confront the tension its own title creates. Elsewhere canon is
  insistent that felling Hometree **does not damage Eywa's network** - the planetary
  mind does not flicker. So which is it: a negligible loss or a catastrophe? The
  honest answer is that both are true, because "important" means two different things
  in ecology, and Hometree is important in the second sense, not the first.
- **STEM payload:** the real, and routinely confused, family of concepts ecologists
  use for "species that matter more than their share": **keystone species** (rare,
  disproportionate, usually trophic - Paine's starfish), **foundation species**
  (abundant, structural, habitat-defining - Dayton's term), **ecosystem engineers**
  (organisms that physically modify habitat - Jones, Lawton & Shachak), **dominant
  species**, and **keystone structures / habitat trees** (a *feature*, not a species,
  that carries disproportionate biodiversity). The payload is the discipline of
  telling these apart, why the distinction changes what a conservationist does, and
  what the empirical record shows when a habitat-forming dominant is removed:
  **American chestnut blight**, **eastern hemlock and the woolly adelgid**,
  **ash dieback / emerald ash borer**, **whitebark pine and Clark's nutcracker**,
  **kelp forests**, **coral reef framework loss**, **mangroves**, **Sphagnum**, the
  documented **global decline of large old trees**, **tree hollows and the centuries
  it takes to make one**, **canopy soils and epiphyte communities**, **figs as
  keystone plant resources**, **co-extinction and extinction debt**, and - to close
  the loop on a tree that is also a clan's whole cosmology - **cultural keystone
  species**.

Research must cover BOTH sides thoroughly and keep them separable. Breadth is
welcome - the author will filter aggressively.

## Part B - Earth-science literature (the ecology of species that matter more than their share)

The real science, from credible/primary sources, at a level a curious non-specialist
can follow. Named studies, numbers, and dates strongly preferred.

1. **The keystone concept, and its original narrowness.** **Robert Paine's** 1966
   *Food web complexity and species diversity* and 1969 coining of **keystone species**
   from the *Pisaster ochraceus* removal experiment at Mukkaw Bay; the arch metaphor and
   what it was meant to capture (**disproportionate effect relative to abundance**).
   Then the crucial qualifier this chapter needs: Paine's keystone was **rare and
   trophic** - it mattered *through consumption*, not through bulk. Cover **how the
   term subsequently inflated** into a catch-all (Mills, Soulé & Doak 1993's critique
   *The keystone-species concept in ecology and conservation*; Power et al. 1996
   *Challenges in the quest for keystones* and their attempt at an operational
   definition - **community importance** measured against a species' proportional
   biomass). Ask explicitly for the argument that **a hugely abundant, structure-forming
   species is NOT a keystone by the original definition**, and what it is instead.
2. **Foundation species - the concept the chapter actually needs.** **Paul Dayton's**
   1972 introduction of **foundation species** from Antarctic benthic work: a
   **locally abundant, often large, structure-forming species that defines the physical
   habitat and stabilises fundamental ecosystem processes**. How it differs from a
   keystone (abundant vs rare; structural/habitat-forming vs trophic; effect
   proportional to bulk vs disproportionate to it) and from a **dominant species**
   (dominant = high biomass; foundation = high biomass *that other species depend on
   structurally*). Ask for the best modern statements of the distinction - e.g.
   **Ellison et al. 2005** *Loss of foundation species: consequences for the structure
   and dynamics of forested ecosystems*, and later reviews (Ellison 2019 on foundation
   species definitions and measurement). Include the acknowledged **messiness** of the
   terminology: many papers use keystone/foundation/dominant loosely, and some authors
   argue the terms should be merged or dropped. Say where the field genuinely disagrees.
3. **Ecosystem engineers.** **Jones, Lawton & Shachak 1994** *Organisms as ecosystem
   engineers*, and the **autogenic** vs **allogenic** distinction (autogenic: the
   organism's own body *is* the structural change - a tree, a coral, a kelp; allogenic:
   it modifies other material - a beaver, an earthworm, a burrowing rodent). The
   chapter needs the autogenic case sharply drawn, because a Hometree is the textbook
   autogenic engineer. Also cover **niche construction** briefly as the evolutionary
   framing, and **keystone structures / habitat trees** (Tews et al. 2004 on keystone
   structures; the literature on **large old trees** as keystone structures - hollows,
   fissured bark, canopy soil - notably **Lindenmayer, Laurance & Franklin 2012**
   *Global decline in large old trees* (Science) and Lindenmayer & Laurance 2017 on
   the ecology and significance of large old trees).

4. **How much life one big tree actually carries.** Hard numbers wherever they exist,
   because the chapter needs to make "a tree is a habitat" quantitative rather than
   poetic:
   - **Epiphyte loads** on single tropical trees (species counts and biomass of
     orchids, bromeliads, ferns, mosses; the classic figures for a single emergent
     tree in Costa Rica/Ecuador), and the **canopy soil / suspended soil** literature
     - how deep it gets, how long it takes to accumulate, what lives in it
     (Nadkarni's canopy-soil work; canopy arthropod inventories).
   - **Arthropod diversity per tree** - **Terry Erwin's** 1982 fogging of *Luehea
     seemannii* in Panama and the resulting (contested) global insect-species
     extrapolation; later canopy-fogging inventories; how many species one tree can
     hold and how many are found on that tree species and no other.
   - **Tree hollows**: how many decades or centuries a cavity takes to form
     (Australian eucalypt figures of 100-200+ years are the standard citation), what
     fraction of vertebrate fauna is hollow-dependent in well-studied systems
     (Australia, Pacific Northwest), and why **a young tree cannot substitute** for an
     old one no matter how many you plant. This is the single most transferable
     Earth lesson for the chapter: **some habitat features can only be produced by
     time**.
   - **Vertebrate use of individual emergent/"habitat" trees**, including the
     literature on **isolated paddock trees** as disproportionate biodiversity
     resources (Manning, Fischer & Lindenmayer 2006 *Scattered trees are keystone
     structures*).
5. **The removal experiments Earth has already run.** For each case, ask for what was
   lost, how fast, what depended on it, whether anything replaced it, and the recovery
   timescale (or the absence of recovery):
   - **American chestnut (*Castanea dentata*) and the blight** (*Cryphonectria
     parasitica*, from ~1904): ~3-4 billion trees lost, up to a quarter of the canopy
     in parts of Appalachia; documented **co-extinctions/declines of dependent moths
     and insects**; the mast-failure consequences for wildlife; the fact that the tree
     is not extinct but functionally gone (resprouting stumps that never mature); a
     century of restoration/backcrossing/transgenic effort.
   - **Eastern hemlock (*Tsuga canadensis*) and the hemlock woolly adelgid**: the
     Harvard Forest work (Ellison et al.) on what a foundation species' loss does to
     microclimate, soil, streams, nitrogen cycling, and the bird/invertebrate
     community - the best-documented **foundation-species loss experiment** in
     temperate forest, including deliberate girdling manipulations.
   - **Ash (*Fraxinus*) - emerald ash borer and ash dieback**: numbers of dependent
     species (the UK figures for ash-associated obligates are frequently cited),
     landscape-scale mortality, and the notion of **functional extinction**.
   - **Whitebark pine (*Pinus albicaulis*) and Clark's nutcracker / grizzly bears** -
     a keystone/foundation case with a tight mutualist dependency and a cascading
     wildlife consequence.
   - **Marine framework losses:** **kelp forests** (*Macrocystis*) as foundation
     species and what urchin barrens look like when they go, including the
     sea-otter/urchin/kelp story only insofar as it distinguishes the *keystone
     predator* (otter) from the *foundation species* (kelp) - this contrast is
     extremely useful to the chapter; **coral reefs** losing structural framework
     after bleaching (structural complexity loss, rugosity decline, fish community
     collapse); **seagrass** and **mangroves** as foundation species; **oyster reefs**;
     **Sphagnum** in peatlands.
   - Ask specifically whether the literature supports a general rule: **does the
     ecosystem reassemble around a different species, or does it change state?**
     Cover **regime shifts / alternative stable states** where relevant, and **whether
     structural loss is more or less reversible than trophic loss**.
6. **Co-extinction, extinction debt, and the lag.** **Co-extinction** of dependent
   species (obligate epiphytes, host-specific insects, parasites and mutualists -
   Koh et al. 2004 on co-extinction and the "coextinction of species affiliates";
   the general point that a host's loss takes its specialists with it) and
   **extinction debt** (Tilman et al. 1994) - the fact that the losses arrive **decades
   to centuries after** the habitat is destroyed, so the immediate aftermath badly
   understates the damage. This gives the chapter its most honest and least
   sentimental point about the felling: the bill is not paid on the day.
7. **Recovery, and what "recovery" means for a structure.** Forest **succession**
   after catastrophic disturbance; **biological legacies** (dead wood, stumps, root
   systems, surviving seed banks) and the Mount St. Helens/Pacific Northwest
   literature on why legacies determine recovery trajectory (Franklin, Swanson); the
   crucial distinction between **regrowing biomass** (fast) and **regrowing structure**
   (slow) - i.e. how long until a forest has hollows, canopy soil, deep bark furrows
   and epiphyte loads again, with real figures from **old-growth vs secondary forest
   comparisons**. Also **nurse logs / coarse woody debris** as the afterlife of a
   giant tree, and how a fallen giant remains a habitat for a century or more - a
   genuinely uplifting and empirically solid note the chapter can land on. And the
   counterpoint: **fire/salvage-logging removal of legacies** and why that is worse
   than the original disturbance.

8. **Cultural keystone species - closing the loop.** The established scientific
   concept of a **cultural keystone species** (Garibaldi & Turner 2004, *Cultural
   keystone species: implications for ecological conservation and restoration*;
   Cristancho & Vining 2004 on "culturally defined keystone species"): a species so
   embedded in a people's diet, material culture, language, and ritual that its loss
   restructures the culture, not merely the ecosystem. Ask for the canonical examples
   (**western red cedar** for Northwest Coast peoples; **salmon**; **camas**;
   **saguaro**; **birch**; sago and others), the criteria proposed for identifying
   one, and the critique of the concept. Also, briefly, **biocultural approaches** and
   why conservation practice now treats ecological and cultural loss as one problem.
   This is how the chapter earns the right to say the Omatikaya's grief is a *finding*,
   not a sentiment.
9. **Good analogies connecting the Earth science back to Pandora.** What an ecologist
   handed the felling of Hometree would immediately say, and where that reasoning would
   strain. Ask the researcher to reason these through and flag weaknesses:
   - That Hometree is textbook **foundation species / autogenic ecosystem engineer /
     keystone structure**, and specifically **not** a keystone species in Paine's
     sense - and that the confusion between those is exactly why "important" gets
     argued about. Whether calling a *single individual tree* a foundation species is
     even legitimate (foundation status is usually a property of a *species*, not an
     individual - ask how the literature handles single-tree cases like scattered
     paddock trees, and whether "keystone structure" is the more defensible label for
     one Hometree).
   - Whether the two canon facts can be reconciled: **the network survives** (a
     topological claim about connectivity) while **the habitat does not** (a structural
     claim about what depends on that physical object). Ask whether Earth ecology has a
     clean parallel - a system where connectivity is robust to the loss of a node that
     nonetheless carried enormous local biodiversity.
   - What a **~20,000-year-old** structure implies about replacement. Compare with
     Earth's slowest-forming habitat features (hollows at 100-200 yr, old-growth
     structure at 200-500 yr, coral framework, peat accumulation rates per century,
     the age of Earth's oldest individual trees and clonal organisms). Ask what
     multiplier this puts on **extinction debt** and whether "irreplaceable on any
     human or Na'vi timescale" is defensible.
   - What the chapter should **predict** about the burn site that canon never shows: who
     colonises first, whether the root system persists as a legacy, whether the hollow's
     obligate residents are already doomed, and what the site looks like in 50 / 500 /
     5,000 years.
   - Where it strains: canon gives essentially no inventory of Hometree's non-Na'vi
     residents, so the entire biodiversity argument is an inference from Earth's
     emergent trees; Pandoran flora may be networked in ways that make individual
     mortality less meaningful; and if Hometrees are common, the loss is local rather
     than systemic - ask which reading the canon numbers actually support.
