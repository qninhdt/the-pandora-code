# Deep Research - Time on Pandora (time-on-pandora)

## Goal

This chapter teaches **two payloads at once**:

- **Pandora payload:** how time is actually *lived* on Pandora as a moon of the
  gas giant **Polyphemus** in the **Alpha Centauri A** system - the length of a
  Pandoran day, the rhythm of **eclipses** as Pandora passes through Polyphemus's
  shadow, the face Pandora keeps (or doesn't) toward its primary, and how
  "seasons" and longer cycles work for a moon whose dominant clock is its orbit
  around a planet rather than around a star. What canon states, implies, or
  leaves open about Pandora's rotation, day/night, eclipse cadence, and seasonal
  variation.
- **STEM payload:** the real orbital mechanics that govern the timekeeping of a
  large moon close to a giant planet - **tidal locking** (synchronous rotation
  and why close-in moons end up tide-locked), **libration** (the slow nodding and
  rocking that lets a tide-locked body show slightly more than half its face),
  **eclipse / transit / occultation geometry**, the difference between a
  **sidereal and a synodic period**, **spin-orbit resonances**, **axial tilt /
  obliquity and what actually drives seasons**, and how a moon's "day" is set by
  its orbit around its planet, not around the star.

Research must cover BOTH sides thoroughly and keep them separable. Breadth is
welcome - the author will filter aggressively.

## Part B - Earth-science literature (tidal locking, libration, orbital mechanics)

The real orbital mechanics and timekeeping science, explained from
credible/primary sources, at a level a curious non-specialist can follow:

1. **Tidal locking / synchronous rotation:** the physical mechanism - tidal
   bulges, dissipation, and torque that drive a close-in moon toward 1:1
   spin-orbit resonance; the **timescale** to become locked and what it depends on
   (distance, mass ratio, rigidity); real examples (Earth's Moon, the Galilean
   moons of Jupiter, Pluto-Charon mutual locking). What "locked" does and does not
   mean for a day on the surface.
2. **Libration:** the apparent nodding/rocking of a tide-locked body - **libration
   in longitude** (from orbital eccentricity, via Kepler's second law),
   **libration in latitude** (from axial tilt relative to the orbit), and
   **diurnal libration** (from the observer's position). Why the Moon shows ~59%
   of its surface over time. Connect to what a tide-locked Pandora would reveal of
   Polyphemus's motion.
3. **Eclipse, transit, and occultation geometry:** the distinction between these
   three; the role of orbital inclination and node alignment; why a close, large
   moon of a giant planet would experience **frequent, regular eclipses** every
   orbit (unlike Earth's rare eclipses); umbra/penumbra, eclipse duration as a
   function of orbital geometry, and the surface temperature drop during a
   planetary-shadow eclipse.
4. **Sidereal vs synodic periods:** the difference between a sidereal day/period
   (relative to the stars) and a synodic one (relative to the Sun/illumination);
   why a tide-locked moon's "day" (sunrise to sunrise) equals its orbital period
   around its planet; worked intuition using Earth's solar vs sidereal day and the
   Moon's sidereal vs synodic month.
5. **Spin-orbit resonances beyond 1:1:** Mercury's **3:2 resonance** and why not
   every body locks 1:1; eccentricity-driven resonances; what conditions favor
   each. (Useful for discussing whether Pandora *must* be 1:1 locked.)
6. **Axial tilt / obliquity and the real cause of seasons:** how seasons arise
   from obliquity (not distance), how a large moon's seasons could be governed by
   the **planet's** orbit around the star plus the moon's own tilt, and how a
   nearby massive companion star (Alpha Centauri B) might perturb climate or
   illumination over longer cycles. Mention Milankovitch-style long-period
   variation briefly.
7. **Illumination environment of a moon close to a gas giant:** "planetshine"
   (reflected light from Polyphemus lighting Pandora's night), the combination of
   primary star + companion star + planet, and how this complicates any simple
   day/night/season picture. Connect to circadian/ecological consequences (sets up
   the night-ecology chapter).
8. Good **analogies** connecting the real orbital mechanics back to "time on
   Pandora" - e.g. what a day, a month, an eclipse, and a year would each
   correspond to physically for a moon of a gas giant, and how an observer would
   tell them apart.
