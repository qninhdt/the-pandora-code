# Deep Research - The bandwidth of a planet (the-bandwidth-of-a-planet)

## Goal

This is the **third chapter of Part III** (after *What Eywa is* and *The
wood-wide web*), and it teaches **two payloads at once**:

- **Pandora payload:** **tsaheylu** (the queue-bond) treated as a literal
  **communication link** - a data channel. The prior two chapters asked *is the
  network a mind?* and *what is the wiring made of?*; this chapter asks the
  engineer's question: **how much information actually crosses the bond, and how
  fast?** Canon shows three escalating "transfers" over the link - real-time
  animal riding (motor + sensory control of a direhorse/ikran), communion with
  ancestral memory at the Tree of Voices/Souls, and, at the limit, the
  **transfer of an entire human consciousness** (Grace's attempted transfer,
  Jake's successful one). Take each as a *payload* moving through a *channel* and
  ask what data-rate it implies. The chapter's spine is the gap between canon's
  awe ("connections outnumber the synapses in a brain") and the engineer's
  sober follow-up: **a huge number of connections is not the same as high
  bandwidth.**
- **STEM payload:** **information theory** - Claude Shannon's framework for
  measuring information in **bits**, the **channel-capacity theorem** (the
  Shannon-Hartley limit C = B·log₂(1 + S/N)), the ideas of **entropy**,
  **noise**, **error-correcting codes**, and the hard ceilings they place on any
  real communication system. The reader should leave able to (a) say what a
  "bit" is and why information is measured that way, (b) understand that **every
  channel has a maximum capacity set by bandwidth and noise**, (c) grasp that
  you cannot send a message faster than the channel's capacity without losing
  information, and (d) apply this to estimate, even crudely, how implausible (or
  not) tsaheylu's biggest feats are - especially **moving a whole mind across a
  neural cable**.

Research must cover BOTH sides thoroughly and keep them cleanly separable.
Breadth is welcome - the author filters aggressively.

## Part B - Earth-science literature (information theory & channel capacity)

The real science, explained from credible/primary sources, at a level a curious
non-specialist can follow. The spine is: *what is information, how is a channel's
maximum capacity set, and what do those limits say about moving huge payloads
through a slow neural link?*

1. **What is a "bit"? Shannon's measure of information (the foundation).**
   Claude Shannon's 1948 paper "*A Mathematical Theory of Communication*" (Bell
   System Technical Journal) - the birth of information theory. Explain
   **information as the resolution of uncertainty**, measured in **bits**: one
   bit = one yes/no answer = a halving of the possibilities. Cover **Shannon
   entropy** (H = -Σ p·log₂ p) intuitively - the average number of bits needed to
   encode a message from a source, and why a predictable source carries less
   information per symbol than a surprising one. Give graspable numbers: a coin
   flip = 1 bit; a letter of English ≈ 1-1.5 bits after redundancy; a page of
   text, an image, a song in bits. This is the unit the whole chapter counts in.

2. **The communication-system model (sender → channel → receiver, plus noise).**
   Shannon's canonical diagram: information source → transmitter (encoder) →
   **channel** → receiver (decoder) → destination, with a **noise source**
   corrupting the channel. Define **channel**, **signal**, **noise**, and
   **bandwidth (B)** plainly. This is the lens the chapter puts over tsaheylu:
   the queue is a *channel*, the bonded mind is the *source*, and whatever
   degrades a biological signal is the *noise*. Establishing this mapping is what
   lets the chapter "measure" a fictional bond at all.

3. **Channel capacity - the Shannon-Hartley theorem (the hard ceiling).** The
   central result: **C = B · log₂(1 + S/N)**, where C is the maximum error-free
   **capacity in bits per second**, B the **bandwidth in hertz**, and S/N the
   **signal-to-noise ratio**. Explain each term and the two big consequences:
   (a) **capacity grows only logarithmically with signal power/SNR** - throwing
   more power at a channel buys surprisingly little extra rate; (b) **capacity
   grows linearly with bandwidth** - bandwidth is the precious resource. State
   the **noisy-channel coding theorem**: below capacity you can communicate with
   *arbitrarily low error* using good codes; **above capacity, error is
   unavoidable** - there is a genuine speed limit, not just an engineering
   inconvenience. Worked mini-examples (a phone line ~3 kHz → tens of kbps; why
   fibre's enormous bandwidth enables Gbps) make the formula concrete.

4. **Error, redundancy, and error-correcting codes.** Why real channels add
   **redundancy** to survive noise: parity, checksums, **error-correcting codes**
   (Hamming codes, and a nod to modern LDPC/turbo codes) that let a receiver
   *detect and repair* corruption. The key idea for the chapter: **a lossless
   transfer of a critical payload (a whole mind) demands either a very clean
   channel or heavy error-correction overhead** - and overhead eats into the
   effective rate. Tie to canon's "transfer a consciousness perfectly": real
   systems guarantee fidelity only by spending capacity on redundancy.

5. **How many bits is a mind / a memory? (estimating the payload).** Give the
   reader credible ranges for the chapter's central calculation, clearly labelled
   as estimates:
   - The human brain's storage, often cited at **~2.5 petabytes** (a popular
     Scientific American figure) or other order-of-magnitude estimates - explain
     these are crude and why.
   - **Synapse count** (~10^14-10^15) and what "storing a connectome" would take
     in bits.
   - The much smaller information *rate* of conscious experience - classic
     psychophysics estimates that the conscious "bottleneck" is only **tens of
     bits per second** even though the senses take in megabits (e.g., the often-
     cited ~10-50 bits/s of conscious throughput vs ~10^7 bits/s at the retina).
   This split - **enormous storage, tiny conscious bandwidth** - is the chapter's
   sharpest tool: it lets the author show that "moving a mind" might mean moving
   petabytes (absurd over a slow link in one night) *or* moving only the slow
   conscious stream (surprisingly feasible) - and canon never says which.

6. **The speed of biological signalling (the real channel's specs).** Ground the
   estimate in neuroscience: **nerve conduction velocity** (~1-120 m/s,
   myelinated vs unmyelinated), the **bandwidth of a single neuron / axon**
   (firing rates, bits per spike), and aggregate estimates for nerves like the
   **optic nerve (~1 Mbit/s per the Koch et al. retina study)** or the corpus
   callosum. Contrast sharply with electronics (light in fibre ~2×10^8 m/s; Gbps-
   Tbps links). This gives the chapter a **realistic ceiling for a neural cable
   like the queue** - and sets up the punchline that an electrochemical bond,
   however many fibres it bundles, is *slow and modest* in bits/s next to a
   copper wire, let alone next to the petabyte payload of a whole mind.

7. **Connection count vs throughput (the core misconception to kill).** Make
   explicit, with information-theory backing, why **a huge number of links does
   not imply high bandwidth or intelligence**: total capacity depends on each
   channel's B and S/N and on how independently the links carry information
   (redundant links add reliability, not proportional capacity). This directly
   answers canon's "connections outnumber the synapses in a brain" - impressive
   *connectivity* says little about *bits per second* or *what is computed*. A
   good place to mention **latency vs bandwidth** (a planet-spanning slow network
   can be high-capacity in total yet hopelessly high-latency for any real-time
   "thought").

8. **Analogies & contrasts that connect the science back to Pandora.** Good
   bridges for the author:
   - **Tsaheylu as a channel** with a finite C = B·log₂(1+S/N) - the single most
     useful reframing: stop asking "is it magic?" and start asking "what's its
     bandwidth?"
   - The **"two readings of mind-transfer"**: petabyte connectome (needs an
     absurd data-rate over one ceremony) vs the **tens-of-bits-per-second
     conscious stream** (a lifetime of *conscious* experience is far smaller and
     might plausibly fit) - a genuinely surprising, defensible insight the chapter
     can land.
   - **Eywa as a storage medium vs a communication channel** - storing every
     ancestor's memory is a *capacity* claim (plausible if the substrate is vast);
     retrieving it *instantly* across the moon is a *bandwidth + latency* claim
     (where Shannon and the speed of biological signalling push back hardest).
   - The **error-correction cost of a lossless soul-copy** - "perfect" transfer
     isn't free; it spends capacity.
   - The **honest verdict** the chapter should be able to reach: tsaheylu as a
     *low-rate, high-meaning* link (feelings, a sensory feed, a slow upload of the
     gist of a life) is consistent with real information theory; tsaheylu as an
     *instant, lossless, planet-wide transfer of complete minds* runs straight
     into Shannon's ceiling and the sluggishness of electrochemistry - the network
     can be vast in connections yet bounded, like everything else, by bits,
     bandwidth, and noise.
