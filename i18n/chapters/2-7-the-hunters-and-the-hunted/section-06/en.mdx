And the most famous Earth case carries a warning Pandora should heed, because the story is usually told too simply. When wolves were returned to Yellowstone in the 1990s after a long absence, the elk they hunted declined, and the young trees the elk had been over-browsing - willow, aspen, cottonwood - began to recover, and from that recovery, the popular telling goes, beavers returned and riverbanks stabilised and even the rivers changed course: a top predator healing a whole landscape from the top down. It is a beautiful story and it is partly true. But careful, sceptical ecology has spent years complicating it, and the complications are the honest part. The elk also declined because of drought and human hunting and the return of bears; the tree recovery was patchy, strong where water was plentiful and absent where it was not; and a great deal of the effect was not wolves *killing* elk at all.

That last point opens the subtlest idea in the whole chapter. A predator changes its prey not only by eating it, but by *frightening* it.

<WhatThisMeans title="Fear does the work the teeth don't">
Long before a hunter catches anything, its mere presence rewrites where and how the prey can live. An animal that knows a predator hunts the open meadow will avoid the open meadow - even when the predator is miles away - feeding instead in worse but safer ground, spending time watching that it could have spent eating, carrying a low chronic dread that suppresses how much it grazes and even how well it breeds. Ecologists call this the <GlossaryTerm slug="landscape-of-fear">landscape of fear</GlossaryTerm>: a map of a habitat drawn not in food but in risk, the prey moving through it as if certain valleys were walled off, because in a sense they are. On Pandora this is everywhere once you look. A sturmbeest herd does not graze the dense ambush-thick forest edge where a thanator could be waiting; it stays in the open where it can see and run, and so the vegetation along that dangerous edge grows untouched - shaped by a predator that never came, regrows because of a fear that was never tested. Often this fear-effect moves more total vegetation than the killing does. The hunter's deadliest tool is frequently not the kill. It is the dread.
</WhatThisMeans>

## The hunter's arithmetic

Step down now from the whole web to the single hunter again, and ask a question the equations skated over: when a viperwolf chooses to chase, what is it actually weighing?

Start with a fact that surprises people who have never watched real predators work: hunters mostly fail. The popular image is of the unstoppable killer, but the truth is closer to the opposite - across Earth's great predators, the success rate of any given hunt tends to run somewhere around one in five, often worse. Most chases end with the prey getting away and the hunter hungry again. That is not incompetence; it is a deep asymmetry written into the contest itself, and it has a name.

<ScientificNote title="The life-dinner principle">
Richard Dawkins and John Krebs put the asymmetry in a single sharp line: the rabbit runs faster than the fox, because the rabbit is running for its life while the fox is only running for its dinner. The stakes are not equal, and so the evolutionary pressure is not equal. A prey animal that fails its test is dead and leaves no offspring; the trait that failed it is erased from the future completely. A predator that fails its test merely goes hungry, tries again tomorrow, and very likely still breeds. So selection presses far harder on the hunted than on the hunter - failure costs the prey *everything* and the predator only a meal - and the result is that prey defences tend to stay one step ahead of predator weapons. This is the <GlossaryTerm slug="life-dinner-principle">life-dinner principle</GlossaryTerm>, and it is why the hexapede's speed and startle-fan and early-warning senses are so good, and why even a superb pack of viperwolves misses more often than it hits. The animal with its life on the line is trying harder, across every generation, than the animal with only its dinner on the line.
</ScientificNote>

This is also the engine of one of biology's great spectacles - the <GlossaryTerm slug="coevolution">coevolutionary</GlossaryTerm> arms race, in which hunter and hunted drive each other to extremes across deep time, each adaptation on one side selecting for a counter on the other. Faster prey select for faster hunters, which select for faster prey still; toxic prey select for toxin-resistant predators, which select for more toxic prey. The cheetah and the gazelle ran each other into the fastest legs on the African plain. On Pandora the same race shows in the viperwolf's cooperative tactics answered by the hexapede's senses and fan, the titanothere's armour answered by the sheer mass of the only two animals that can crack it. Neither side ever wins. That is the point: the race is the equilibrium.

Given that hunting is costly and often fails, a predator cannot afford to chase everything. It must choose - and the choosing follows a logic clean enough to write as a rule. A hunter should pursue a prey type only when the energy it expects to gain outruns the energy it would spend finding and catching it; the moment a richer or easier target is common enough to be worth holding out for, the predator should ignore the poorer one and wait. <GlossaryTerm slug="optimal-foraging-theory">Optimal foraging theory</GlossaryTerm> is the name for this accounting, and it is not a metaphor - predators really do behave, on the whole, as if running the sums, expanding their diet when good prey is scarce and narrowing it when good prey is plentiful.

One consequence matters enough to pull out on its own, because it loops straight back to the stability we have been chasing. When a predator's favourite prey grows scarce, the predator does not keep hunting it to the last individual - that would cost too much effort for too little return. Instead it *switches*, turning its attention to whatever is now more abundant and easier to find. This <GlossaryTerm slug="prey-switching">prey switching</GlossaryTerm> is precisely the behaviour behind the stabilising Type III response from earlier: rare prey is spared not out of mercy but out of arithmetic, because chasing a vanishing target stops being worth it, and the predator's gaze slides to easier meals. Selfish accounting at the level of the single hunter becomes, at the level of the whole forest, the mechanism that keeps rare species from being hunted into oblivion. The forest's stability is built, in part, out of predators declining to bother.
